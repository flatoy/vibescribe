Five ways to settle in.

Every direction keeps the overlay and language picker from today and changes where everything else lives. Each one is shown in the same states (ready, first launch, needs attention, plus one screen specific to it) so you can compare them directly. Overlay variants are at the end and work with any direction.

My pick: B, with A’s menu bar menu. You use a push-to-talk app by holding a key, not by opening a window. Settings should fit on one screen you rarely visit, and the menu bar menu (last transcript, languages, history) handles daily needs. Go with C instead if you find yourself reusing old transcripts often.

Direction A · from the first page

ASidebardark glass · 780 × 560 · six pages

A page per topic, the way System Settings works. It holds everything, and problems show as a banner and a sidebar dot. All states for A are on the first page.

Good
  • Room to grow
  • Familiar layout
  • Diagnostics stay out of the way
Costs
  • The most navigation for a small app
  • Setup needs its own separate window
Direction B

BCompactdark glass · 476 wide · one screen

Everything you’d change is on one screen, top to bottom, in the order people care about. The model, history retention and logs are folded under a single disclosure. First launch uses the same window, with a checklist at the top.

Good
  • Nothing to navigate
  • Setup and settings are the same place, so nothing to learn twice
  • Smallest to build
Costs
  • History has to live in the menu bar menu
  • Each new feature competes for space
Direction C

CJournaldark glass · 880 × 590 · transcripts first

Opening the app shows what you’ve said, not a form. Each dictation is a card showing where it went, with Copy and Paste again. Settings are a sheet over the list, because you rarely need them.

Good
  • The window has a reason to be opened
  • Recovers text lost to the wrong window
  • Shows the app working in real time
Costs
  • Needs reliable local storage and search
  • Some people don’t want their dictations kept
Direction D

DMenu bar onlypopover · 310 wide · no window

Clicking the menu bar icon opens a Control Center–style panel with three tabs. Language tiles work like toggles. For a menu bar app this is the most at-home option, but the smallest canvas.

Good
  • Everything is one click from anywhere
  • No Dock icon or window to manage
  • Language switching is a single click on a tile
Costs
  • Popovers close when you click away, which hurts during permission setup
  • Vocabulary and logs feel cramped
Direction E

ENative lightsystem appearance · toolbar tabs · form layout

A deliberately conventional option: Safari-style toolbar tabs, right-aligned labels and system blue, following the Mac’s light or dark mode. It’s a baseline for judging whether the custom look is worth it.

Good
  • Instantly familiar
  • Follows light and dark mode for free
  • Standard SwiftUI Settings scene with little custom code
Costs
  • Loses the spectrum identity
  • Clashes with the always-dark overlay and picker
Works with any direction

Overlay variants

You said you like the current overlay, so variant 1 keeps it. The other three change where recording feedback appears. Each row shows the same moments: listening, transcribing, and done.

1 · Pill (today’s overlay, with waveform)

Floats below the menu bar. Familiar, readable, and has room for hints.

2 · Island

A black shape that grows out of the top edge, around the notch on newer MacBooks. It looks like part of the hardware rather than a floating window. On Macs without a notch it hangs from the menu bar.

3 · At the cursor

A small bubble next to the text cursor, where you’re already looking. It uses Accessibility to find the cursor and falls back to the pill when an app doesn’t report it.

4 · Edge glow

The quietest option: no box, just a thin spectrum line along the top of the screen that breathes with your voice. Nothing covers your content. The trade-off is less room for words, so errors still need the pill.
